Active Inference on a JEPA Map

H‑JEPA gives System 2 a latent space. The RGM gives it a generative model. Full active inference means every decision the agent makes comes out of that one model through two quantities: variational free energy \(F\) for perception, learning and structure, and expected free energy \(G\) for choice. A JEPA trained without stop‑gradient (Slow SVReg) has the geometry that makes the model valid and keeps both free energies in closed form. Its places are Gaussian, its moves act like translations, and distance to a goal measures progress.

Built from: Ren et al. 2026 (Content–Geometry tradeoff), Friston et al. 2024 (Renormalising generative models), Friston et al. 2025 (Active inference and artificial reasoning), and your repo: DESIGN_ANIMAL.md, DESIGN_ACTION_BUNDLE.md, the 26 Nov notes, system2/rgm_curious.py.

Version 2. Checked claim by claim against the papers, the repo, recomputation and 15 further papers. Eight errors are fixed, eight claims are reworded and eleven hold unchanged. See section 10.

The short answer

  1. Treat the whitened \(z_3\) as the observation and put discrete places above it with a Gaussian likelihood \(P(z\mid s)=\mathcal N(\mu_s,\Sigma_s)\). Smooth geometry is what makes that likelihood valid.
  2. Turn every hand-set rule in decide() into a term of one \(G\): risk, ambiguity, novelty, model information gain, and a reachability cost at the planning horizon. The modes and thresholds go away.
  3. Dreams become Dirichlet prior mass, never counts. Your rule R1 ("plan only on seen evidence") is then just Bayes' rule. An unconfirmed edge looks informative (high novelty) and unreliable (low expected‑log reach) at the same time.
  4. Sleep is Bayesian model reduction. "Is this a wall?", "are these one place?", "is this a new world?" become hypotheses. Awake, the agent picks the moves that best tell them apart (active reasoning).
  5. Action is control as inference. The reflex minimises a precision-weighted error to the predicted place, using that place's own covariance as the precision.

1Why JEPA geometry matters to active inference

Ren et al. eliminate the optimal linear predictor from a linear JEPA (their Theorem 2.1, stated without the action term; Section A.3 of their paper adds the action). In their notation the loss is \(\operatorname{tr}S_{11}-\operatorname{tr}(C^{\top}S^{-1}C)\). Below, \(\hat{\mathbb E}\) stands for that best linear prediction. Without stop‑gradient, the encoder is penalised for the target variance the predictor cannot explain. SVReg adds a floor on the spread of every random slice, so the encoder cannot cheat by shrinking:

\[ \mathcal L_{\text{no-sg}}=\underbrace{\operatorname{tr}\operatorname{Var}[z_{t+1}]}_{\text{target variance}}-\underbrace{\operatorname{tr}\operatorname{Var}\!\big[\hat{\mathbb E}(z_{t+1}\mid z_t,a_t)\big]}_{\text{explained by the predictor}}, \qquad \mathcal R_{\text{SVReg}}=\mathbb E_{v\sim\mathcal U(S^{d-1})}\Big[\max\!\big(0,\,1-\operatorname{std}(v^{\top}z)\big)^2\Big] \]

With the scale fixed by the floor, minimising unexplained variance means keeping the directions that can be predicted from the past and the action, and dropping the rest (noise). For linear‑Gaussian dynamics that is the same as keeping the directions with the most predictive information. On a whitened direction with lag correlation \(\rho\), the unexplained variance is \(1-\rho^2\) and the predictive information is \(-\tfrac12\ln(1-\rho^2)\). Both depend only on \(\rho\), so both pick the same subspace, the top canonical directions. Creutzig & Sprekeler (2008) show the same link between slowness and predictive information. Littwin et al. (2024) find a related bias in deep linear JEPAs with stop‑gradient: they favour features with high predictive influence. The RGM paper's first renormalisation step does the same job in space. It takes the SVD of pixel blocks because, for a linear map, the leading singular vectors maximise mutual information (their Eq. 10). So H‑JEPA can serve as the bottom of the RGM, a learned coarse-graining over time that keeps the slow, relevant variables. The discrete RGM is built on top of it.

2The generative model, all of it

Every tensor in the RGM paper's Figure 1 has a concrete meaning here:

\[ \begin{aligned} \textbf{A}:\quad &P(z_t\mid s_t{=}i)=\mathcal N(z_t;\,\mu_i,\Sigma_i), &&(\mu_i,\Sigma_i)\sim\mathrm{NIW}(m_0,\kappa_0,\Psi_0,\nu_0)\\ \textbf{B}:\quad &P(s_{t+1}{=}i\mid s_t{=}j,k_t{=}k)=\theta_{ijk}, &&\theta_{\cdot jk}\sim\mathrm{Dir}(\mathbf b_{\cdot jk}),\ \ \mathbf b=\mathbf b^{\text{prior}}+\mathbf n\\ \textbf{D, E}:\quad &P(s_0,\,k_{1:L}\mid r)=\mathbf D_{r}\otimes\mathbf E_{r} &&\text{a route sets the first place and the move plan}\\ \textbf{C}:\quad &\ln P(z\mid \mathbf C)=\ln\mathcal N(z;\,z_g,\Sigma_g) &&\text{goal preference over the latent}\\ &\ln\tilde C_i=\ln\!\textstyle\int\!\mathcal N(z;\mu_i,\Sigma_i)\,\mathcal N(z;z_g,\Sigma_g)\,dz &&\text{chosen place prior}\\ &\phantom{\ln\tilde C_i}=\ln\mathcal N(\mu_i;\,z_g,\Sigma_i{+}\Sigma_g)+\text{const}\\ \textbf{E},\gamma:\quad &P(\pi)=\sigma\big(\ln\mathbf E_\pi-\gamma\,\mathbf G_\pi\big), &&\gamma=1/\beta,\ \ \beta\sim\Gamma(1,\beta_0) \end{aligned} \]
  • \(o_t=z_t\): the whitened level‑3 latent at each level‑3 tick (PaperRGM's whitening).
  • \(k\): a move, a cluster of executed upper actions in the log‑signature group from DESIGN_ACTION_BUNDLE.md. Moves are what \(\mathbf B\) is indexed by, which a Beta "edge made / failed" model cannot express.
  • \(\mathbf b^{\text{prior}}\) comes from geometry and dreams (section 5). \(\mathbf n\) is experience. The two memories of DESIGN_ANIMAL ("seen" and "dreamed") are exactly these two terms.
  • A second outcome modality fits naturally: \(o^{\text{body}}\in\{\text{moving},\text{stuck},\text{flipped}\}\) from torso height and orientation (R7), with \(\mathbf C\) strongly averse to the last two. The stuck reflex then follows from preferences.
  • \(\tilde C\) is a choice, not a consequence. It reads the preference as "the chance that place \(i\) emits a goal‑like \(z\)", the optimality-variable view of control as inference (Levine 2018). The other choice, \(\mathbb E\ln\mathcal N\), adds a penalty \(-\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_g^{-1}\Sigma_i)\) on broad places: −1.82 nats against −1.44 in a 1‑D check. Pick one and keep it.
  • Nothing here is a policy network. The whole of System 2 is counts, Gaussians and two free energies.

3Perception and learning: minimise \(F\)

State inference. Replace the nearest‑place assignment with a Bayesian filter over places. It combines three messages, as in Eq. 4 of both Friston papers. It is a forward filter: the backward (smoothing) message is dropped, which the RGM paper allows for online use.

\[ \begin{aligned} \ln Q(s_t{=}i)&=\underbrace{\ln\mathcal N(z_t;\mu_i,\Sigma_i)}_{\text{bottom-up }(\mathbf A)}+\underbrace{\textstyle\sum_j Q(s_{t-1}{=}j)\,\overline{\ln\theta}_{ij k_{t-1}}}_{\text{from the last hop }(\mathbf B)}+\underbrace{\ln P(s_t{=}i\mid r)}_{\text{top-down }(\mathbf D,\mathbf E)}-\ln Z_t\\[4pt] \overline{\ln\theta}_{ijk}&=\psi(b_{ijk})-\psi\Big(\textstyle\sum_{i'}b_{i'jk}\Big) \end{aligned} \]

The free energy of each tick is the agent's running surprise:

\[ F_t=\underbrace{\mathrm{KL}\big[Q(s_t)\,\big\|\,P(s_t\mid s_{t-1},k_{t-1},r)\big]}_{\text{complexity}}-\underbrace{\mathbb E_{Q(s_t)}\ln\mathcal N(z_t;\mu_{s},\Sigma_{s})}_{\text{accuracy}} \]

A sustained rise in \(F_t\) means the current map no longer explains the stream. Compare \(F\) under each map in the library and switch to the best (section 6). That replaces the hand-built "surprise spike" rule for new‑world detection.

Learning (soft counts).

\[ n_{ijk}\leftarrow n_{ijk}+w\;Q(s_{t+1}{=}i)\,Q(s_t{=}j),\qquad w=\tfrac{1}{\text{stride}}\ \text{ for sliding-window pairs} \]

The sliding window (R4) yields 20 overlapping pairs for each real hop. Counting each pair as 1 makes the agent 20× too sure of its edges. That is the same disease as imagined counts, only milder. Weighting by \(1/20\) keeps the coverage and the mean transition while the concentration stays honest. Whether it keeps the 13/13‑vs‑6/13 benefit is an open question. If that benefit came from coverage, it stays. If it came partly from overconfidence, it shrinks, and lower counts also cut the expected‑log reach mass, so deadlines look harder. Rerun the risk screen with \(w=1\) and \(w=1/20\) before choosing. The outer product of marginals is the mean‑field count update used in SPM and pymdp.

The place parameters \((\mu_i,\Sigma_i)\) get the conjugate NIW update, weighted by \(Q(s_t{=}i)\).

Growing a place. Use the Chinese‑restaurant rule of a Dirichlet‑process mixture (Neal 2000), taken greedily. A latent opens a candidate place when the prior predictive of a new cluster beats every existing place's posterior predictive:

\[ \ln\alpha+\ln t\big(z_t;\,\text{NIW prior predictive}\big)\;>\;\max_i\Big[\ln n_i+\ln t\big(z_t;\,\text{place }i\text{'s NIW posterior predictive}\big)\Big] \]

Both sides are Student‑t densities. Swapping the right side for \(\mathcal N(z;\mu_i,\Sigma_i)\) is a cheaper approximation that overstates how sure young places are. The candidate waits in the new‑place buffer until sleep accepts it with the RGM's model‑growth test (its Eq. 9: log odds \(=\Delta F+\Delta G>0\)). Places are never grown on the spot, which keeps rule R3.

4Choice: one expected free energy

Today's decide() picks a mode. If a goal is reachable, it scores \(\ln q+\eta\,\mathrm{IG}\). Otherwise it uses discounted information gain. A habit bonus, an ig_eps cutoff and a "still go" fallback sit on top. In full active inference there is a single \(G\). It is evaluated by a recursion in the style of sophisticated inference (Friston et al. 2021), approximated at the level of places, with counterfactual count updates as in sophisticated learning (Hodson et al. 2023):

\[ \begin{aligned} G_d(s,k;\mathbf b)=\;&\underbrace{\mathrm{KL}\big[\bar\theta_{\cdot sk}\,\big\|\,\tilde C\big]}_{\text{risk}} +\underbrace{\textstyle\sum_{s'}\bar\theta_{s'sk}\,\alpha_{s'}}_{\text{ambiguity}} -\underbrace{I_{\mathrm{Dir}}(\mathbf b_{\cdot sk})}_{\text{novelty}} -\underbrace{[d{=}1]\,I_{sk}(m)}_{\text{model IG, next move only}}\\ &+\underbrace{\textstyle\sum_{s'}\bar\theta_{s'sk}\sum_{k'}Q(k'\mid s')\,G_{d+1}\big(s',k';\,\mathbf b+\mathbf e_{s'sk}\big)}_{\text{the future, counts updated along the branch}}\\[8pt] Q(k'\mid s')=\;&\sigma\big(\ln E_{k'}-\gamma\,G_{d+1}(s',\cdot)\big)_{k'},\qquad \bar\theta=\mathbf b/b_0\\[2pt] \alpha_{s}=\;&\mathbb E_{z\sim\mathcal N(\mu_s,\Sigma_s)}\,H\big[Q(\tilde s\mid z)\big]\in[0,\ln K],\qquad G_{H+1}(s)=-\ln V_{T-H}(s) \end{aligned} \]

Three corrections from the second check.

  • Ambiguity is measured in the same units as risk. Version 1 used the Gaussian entropy \(h_s=\tfrac12\ln\lvert2\pi e\Sigma_s\rvert\). That is the textbook term, but it is measured in latent units and grows with dimension. In 32 dimensions, a place 2× wider per axis costs 22 more nats, while risk over 64 places tops out near \(\ln 64=4.2\). So ambiguity would decide everything. \(\alpha_s\) asks instead how confusable place \(s\) is with its neighbours. It is 0 for a well‑separated place and about \(\ln 2\) for a clone pair. It is unit‑free and estimated by Monte Carlo after each refit. It is still the "expected entropy given the state" term of \(G\), with the observation taken as the filtered place label rather than raw \(z\). That is an approximation.
  • Novelty updates along the branch. Version 1 reused the same \(\mathbf b\) at every depth, so a plan that crosses an untested edge four times got four times its novelty: 1.59 nats against 0.89 nats once the counts are updated. Passing \(\mathbf b+\mathbf e_{s'sk}\) down each branch is exact, because the recursion already branches on the landing place.
  • Model information gain counts at the first move only. Friston et al. 2025 say it "does not lend itself to evaluation over sequences of actions" unless the parameters are updated in the imagined future. They restrict it to the next action, and so does the recursion now.

The novelty term is closed form; I checked it against Monte Carlo to within 0.001 nats. Your beta_bernoulli_ig is the two‑outcome case of:

\[ I_{\mathrm{Dir}}(\mathbf b)=H[\bar\theta]-\mathbb E_{\theta\sim\mathrm{Dir}(\mathbf b)}H[\theta] =-\sum_i\bar\theta_i\ln\bar\theta_i-\psi(b_0+1)+\sum_i\bar\theta_i\,\psi(b_i+1) \]

The leaf cost \(-\ln V_{T-H}\) is your reach_prob recursion, with two changes. It runs on the expected‑log transitions \(\exp\overline{\ln\theta}\), left unnormalised, and on the time actually left, \(T\), instead of a discount \(\lambda\). The missing mass \(1-\sum_i\exp\overline{\ln\theta}_{ijk}\) counts as no progress. This leaf is a design choice of mine, not something taken from the papers. Friston's inductive inference (RGM Figs. 26–27; Friston et al. 2025, Neural Computation 37(4)) works backwards from the goal under precise transitions. It gives a high cost to places that cannot reach the goal in time. The geometric mean \(\exp\overline{\ln\theta}\) is what the papers use in state‑inference messages. Reusing it as a reach probability penalises unconfirmed edges for a deadline, which is what R1 wants, but it is a heuristic. A posterior lower quantile of \(\theta\) would do the same job. Test both.

Why this is affordable, and what it costs. True sophisticated inference searches over sequences of belief states (Friston et al. 2021). Here the recursion branches over places, which is accurate only when a precise \(\mathbf A\) collapses \(Q(s)\) after every hop. With \(K=64\), about 16 moves and \(H=4\), the tree has about \((64\cdot16)^4\) leaves before pruning. It is affordable because \(\bar\theta_{\cdot sk}\) is sparse: about 2–3 landing places matter per move. Prune branches below 1% probability and the tree stays small. Updating counts along a branch rules out plain memoisation over \((s,d)\). A practical hybrid: update counts for the first two levels, then switch to the memoised version (counts frozen), which is a \(K\times16\times H\) table. That is the second dividend of good geometry: low ambiguity makes the search cheap.

Precision. \(\gamma\) is inferred. Under the process theory (Friston et al. 2017; Hesp et al. 2021 call this update "affective charge"), the fixed point is \(\beta=\beta_0+(\boldsymbol\pi_{\text{post}}-\boldsymbol\pi_{\text{prior}})^{\top}\mathbf G\). Sign check: if evidence shifts belief toward low‑\(G\) policies, the product is negative, \(\beta\) falls and \(\gamma\) rises. So confidence grows when outcomes agree with the plan and falls when hops fail. Low \(\gamma\) flattens \(Q(k)\), and the agent hedges and explores. Friston 2025's Fig. 4 "confidence" panel plots a related but different quantity: the negative entropy of the policy posterior. Log both.

Handing over is a policy. Add an option \(h\), "let H‑JEPA's gradient planner go for \(z_g\)". Give its success a Beta per bin of predicted time‑to‑goal (head C4). Its \(G\) is risk plus negative novelty, and it competes in the same softmax. The threshold \(\theta\) of handover_v disappears, and the agent learns where H‑JEPA beats the map.

Travelling to the frontier (R5, E2) also comes out of \(G\). At the route level, a policy "go to place \(f\) on seen edges, then try its exits" has low travel risk and high novelty at \(f\). Depth in the hierarchy does what a separate "explore there" mode would have done.

5Dreams are priors: rule R1 is Bayes' rule

Three results point the same way: sleep‑dreaming (24 Nov), F3 imagined counts (11/13 → 4/13) and the life run. Imagination added to the map as if it were experience makes planning worse. The principled fix is to put imagination into the prior of \(\mathbf B\), with a small total mass. The geometry supplies that prior:

\[ b^{\text{prior}}_{s'sk}=\omega_0(\text{region})\cdot\operatorname{softmax}_{s'}\Big[\ln\mathcal N\big(\mu_{s'};\ \hat z_{sk},\ \Sigma_{s'}{+}\Sigma_{F}\big)\Big] \] \[ \hat z_{sk}=\begin{cases}F_3\big(\mu_s,\,h(g_k)\big) & \text{free dream}\\[2pt] z'+F_3\big(z,h(g_k)\big)-F_3\big(z,h(g_{k_0})\big) & \text{counterfactual, from a tried move }k_0\\[2pt] (\bar{\mathbf B}_{k_2}\bar{\mathbf B}_{k_1})_{\cdot s}\ \text{(two-hop product)} & \text{composed move }g_k=g_{k_1}g_{k_2}\ (\text{BCH})\end{cases} \]

Fit \(\omega_0\) by empirical Bayes rather than by hand. Maximise the Dirichlet–multinomial evidence of the edges actually tested in each region, over a grid of \(\omega_0\). This replaces the trust kernel, and it is the same "trust learned per region from tests" idea (R9), made exact. Here is what an edge with dream confidence 0.8 looks like at \(\omega_0=0.25\):

6Sleep is model reduction, waking is active reasoning

Bayesian model reduction scores any reduced prior \(\boldsymbol\beta'_m\) from the full prior \(\boldsymbol\beta\) and the posterior \(\mathbf b\) alone, with no refitting (Friston et al. 2018; RGM Eq. 8). Here \(\mathrm B(\cdot)\) is the multivariate beta function:

\[ \Delta F_m=\ln P(o\mid m)-\ln P(o\mid m_{\text{full}})=\ln\mathrm B(\mathbf b'_m)-\ln\mathrm B(\boldsymbol\beta'_m)-\ln\mathrm B(\mathbf b)+\ln\mathrm B(\boldsymbol\beta), \qquad \mathbf b'_m=\mathbf b+\boldsymbol\beta'_m-\boldsymbol\beta \]

Sign convention: positive \(\Delta F_m\) favours the reduced model. RGM Eq. 8 writes the same quantity with the opposite sign. I checked the formula numerically against the Dirichlet–multinomial evidence computed directly; the two agree to four decimals. It is exact for any alternative Dirichlet prior, not only sparser ones. Only the first two of your questions are reductions of a single Dirichlet. The other two need their own exact scores, which are just as cheap:

Sleep applies the razor. Awake, the agent goes looking for the evidence that discriminates. Following Friston et al. 2025 (Eqs. 10–12), the posterior over hypotheses and the expected information gain of trying move \(k\) at \(s\) are:

\[ Q(m\mid o)=\sigma\big(\ln P(m)+\Delta F_m\big)_m, \qquad I_{sk}(m)=\sum_{s'}\underbrace{\textstyle\sum_{m}Q(m)\,\bar\theta^{(m)}_{s'sk}}_{\text{model-averaged prediction}}\;\mathrm{KL}\Big[Q\big(m\mid \mathbf b+\mathbf e_{s'sk}\big)\,\Big\|\,Q\big(m\mid\mathbf b\big)\Big] \]

The predictive weight is averaged over models, as the paper specifies for its Eq. 11. Version 1 used the full model's \(\bar\theta\), which is slightly off. Each updated \(Q(m\mid\mathbf b+\mathbf e)\) costs one ratio per model: adding one count changes \(\Delta F_m\) by \(\ln(b'_{s'}/b'_0)-\ln(b_{s'}/b_0)\).

Commit (the "Occam's razor" step) when \(\ln\frac{Q(m^*)}{1-Q(m^*)}\) passes a high threshold. The paper uses 16 nats, at which point the winner is at least \(e^{16}\) times more likely. Then replace \(\mathbf b\) by the selected model's posterior. The paper multiplies those counts by a large number, such as 512, to switch off novelty about a settled question (its Eq. 13). In the three‑ball task, dropping the model and parameter information-gain terms raised the mean time to discover the rule from 19.4 to 28.5 trials and cut the score from 150 to 89.2. Dropping all information gain left the rule undiscovered after 64 trials in about half the games, and the score fell to 51. The paper also reports one premature commitment to the wrong model, so 16 nats is not a guarantee.

Growing structure is the mirror image (RGM Eq. 9). Accept an augmented model when \(\ln\frac{P(m'\mid o)}{P(m\mid o)}=\Delta F+\Delta G>0\), where \(\Delta G\) is the change in the expected free energy of the parameters. New places, new moves and new routes all enter this way. One more RGM idea fits here: active learning (its Eqs. 5–7), where each count update is itself accepted only if it lowers the expected free energy of the parameters. It is optional. Try it if window noise keeps blurring edges.

7Action is control as inference

The RGM agent "predicts its own future and the reflex fulfils it" (RGM paper, Planning as inference). The reflex should minimise the free energy of the predicted outcome, a Gaussian place, not a point:

\[ a^{*}_{1:T}=\arg\min_{a_{1:T}}\ \tfrac12\big(\hat z_3(a)-\mu_{s'}\big)^{\top}\big(\gamma_{\text{ref}}\Sigma_{s'}\big)^{-1}\big(\hat z_3(a)-\mu_{s'}\big) \]
  • An elongated corridor place tolerates error along the corridor and punishes error across it. Plain L2 to an exemplar treats both directions the same.
  • Smooth geometry is what keeps this cost well behaved along gradient descent.
  • The reflex's fast learning (AdaJEPA, JEPA‑TTT) is parameter learning at the fastest timescale. OpTTA's optimism term enters the reflex objective only when \(\mathbf C\) is informative (goal mode). In AIF terms, the preference reaches the body, so optimism stays anchored to what was observed.

update NIW(mu, Sigma) with responsibilities Q_s

What could make this wrong

  • Places are not Gaussian in the release \(z_3\). A jaggedness of 2.2 suggests folds. Check the held-out log-likelihood of per-place Gaussians against a mixture with more components per place. If it fails, T1 comes before A1.
  • Novelty overwhelms risk in a large map. Many unvisited edges can add up. Updating counts along each branch removes the double counting, and \(\gamma\) and the finite horizon \(T\) bound the rest. Watch the goal success rate while the map grows.
  • \(G\) itself is a modelling commitment. Millidge, Tschantz & Buckley (2021) show that the expected free energy is not simply variational free energy carried into the future, and that the exploration it produces comes from how it is defined. "Full active inference" here means using the standard \(G\) consistently, with every term derived from one model. It does not mean \(G\) is the only principled objective. Sophisticated inference reduces to Bayes‑adaptive RL in the zero‑temperature limit (Friston et al. 2021). That gives a non‑AIF baseline to compare against.
  • Collapsing the belief after each hop is wrong in aliased places. When \(H[Q(s)]\) is high, branch on the top two places instead of one.
  • Empirical Bayes for \(\omega_0\) needs tested dream edges. Early in a life there are none, so start \(\omega_0\) low (0.1–0.25 of a real try).
